I was never big on New Year's Resolutions.[^abbr] I'm still not. But some way, somehow, I made one last December: read 50 books in a year.

[^abbr]: Henceforth, "NYR."
## The Trial

The choice of number was somewhat arbitrary. I thought a book a week would be nice and easy to keep track of, but would I really be able to keep up that pace? I realized if I gave myself two weeks off, I would have 50 books instead of 52, and 50 is a very appealing number.[^fifty] So, 50 it was. I was even able to give myself a bit of a head start because I was in the middle of reading some books when the ball dropped.[^cheating]

[^fifty]: 50 is the smallest number that is the sum of two non-zero square numbers in two distinct ways[^sum]

[^sum]: 49 plus 1, and 25 plus 25.

[^cheating]: I suppose technically what I'm counting is books _finished_ and not books _read_. Is that cheating? Whatever, it's my NYR. I get to make the rules.

Unlike every other NYR I've made in my contiguous 23 years of being, I've been keeping up with this one. Present tense.[^tense] It's been the ideal NYR experience for me: challenging, yet fun and fulfilling. I even added two sub-resolutions, looser goals I didn't have to always meet: try to keep a fiction book in rotation, and try to keep an audiobook in rotation.

[^tense]: Present perfect continuous, but hey who's counting?

"Rotation" means exactly what you think it does; to put even more things on my plate, I wanted to try reading multiple books at once, inspired by the following quote.

> “I am reading six books at once, the only way of reading; since, as you will agree, one book is only a single unaccompanied note, and to get the full sound, one needs ten others at the same time.”
> 
> --Virginia Woolf

It would seem that _I'm_ afraid of Virginia Woolf, because that's a damn big number.[^six] At first I tried doing the same, but she probably meant books that were about similar things. It was difficult to hold _A History of the Philippines_ and _The Brothers Karamazov_ in conversation (both of which are on hiatus, so to speak), and I ended up being one chapter deep into seven different books. After watching [a YouTube video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JdatH4vRQFc) about getting through your backlog, I followed their advice and brought my rotation size down to three.

[^six]: The notion of "damn big" is, of course, relative. If we're talking about [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_number), six is actually the smallest.

Within a couple of months I fell into a rhythm. I'd finish a book in about a week, reading maybe an hour before bed, and write it down on my phone or laptop. I'd listen to audiobooks while on my morning walk. I read books gathering dust on my shelves and books hot off the presses. For more than half the year, nearly three-quarters, I kept this rhythm up.

Past tense.
## Paradise Lost

At the start of the year, the desire to read more books coincided with another desire: the desire to organize my Notes app.

It isn't a good idea to give me an infinitely tall stack of sticky notes. I jot down pretty much anything. Random thoughts I had just before falling asleep, a million half-baked to-do lists, restaurant recommendations, first drafts of fanfiction, songs, passwords, links, whatever.

Being former president of the TUGSLUG,[^tugslug] I'm always conscious of my over-reliance on proprietary technology. If I'm just using the Notes app like a glorified stack of Post-its, why must I do it on Tim Apple's terms? There's nothing special about the Notes app keeping me locked into the Apple ecosystem. I should use something else to jot down my notes, something more Linuxish.

[^tugslug]: Tufts University GNU/Linux User Group.[^slash] Also known more commonly and more boringly as the Linux Club.

[^slash]: The S comes from the "slash" in "GNU/Linux."

Naturally, I turned to Obsidian. I was drawn to its simplicity, robustness, integration with technology, and price (or lack thereof). Despite it not being FOSS, it's quite popular in FOSS circles. It even has Vim keybindings. And I didn't use it in the crazy, technical, productive way like a Second Brain or a Zettelkasten or a personal wiki. I used it as a Notes app, but this time with folders. Just another glorified stack of Post-its.

Though it's free, Obsidian does offer a paid service called Obsidian Sync which lets you (surprise, surprise) sync your notes between devices. But since Obsidian stores notes as plain Markdown files, all you need is a file-sharing service like Dropbox to get the same effect. I decided for the sake of simplicity to use iCloud.

Sometimes the simplest solution is the best one. But that also means there are times when it isn't. There are even times when the simplest solution is the _worst_ one. What I'm getting at is  some way, somehow, the file I was using to keep track of all the books I had read this year got overwritten.

When I realized what had happened, swarms of would'ves and could'ves and should'ves buzzed inside my head. I should've paid for Obsidian Sync. I should've used git to do version control. I should've used a different app. I should've used Orgmode. I could've used Goodreads, or StoryGraph, or BookWyrm, or something.

But I'm not so sure that would've fixed things.

Was I sad? Of course. I lost all my progress towards reaching both a goal and the end of the year. I lost also a memento, a gift for my future self. A gift that got lost in transit. I was looking forward to looking back.

But the real sting was something else. The real sting was that betrayal, that grim reminder that technology, like all things, is fallible.
## Snow Crash

Technology has a [a very strange patchwork](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BSeebz9LQRc) of memory. Sometimes photographic, sometimes like a goldfish.[^ram]

[^ram]: Talk about random access.

I always remember being told in school to be careful what I put on the internet, because it'll stay there forever. But that's not true. Try looking up a solution to any technical problem you have and reading through a Reddit thread full of redacted comments solving it, then tell me how forever the internet is. This u/\[deleted\] guy must know his stuff, I see his replies everywhere!

Tom Scott says this point better than I could in his greatest video, a video that I cannot name. I'm referring to his most viewed video, titled "This Video Has _x_[^x] Views." It bothers me how the only thing the comments say is omg its a miracle the title is still so accurate lol. **The point is that some day it won't be.** 

He filmed himself before the White Cliffs of Dover which will one day be washed away. The whole video is about the things that were built in the early days of the internet, how they can't be built anymore, and how what has been built is eroding away too. The internet? Forever? It's anything but. Things change. Things vanish. Things break.

[^x]: At the time of publishing this blogpost, _x_ = 77,168,730. And yes, it's still accurate. 

The clip of _Dan Vs._ linked above is a prime example of such ephemerality. Despite all the times I've watched this scene on YouTube, this was the only clip of it I could find. All those previous uploads are gone. And who knows when this clip will join them.

[So is it any wonder people are afraid of technology?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fc1P-AEaEp8)
## The Library of Babel

There are, of course, books I remember reading. Most recently _The Art of War_ by Sun Tzu, translated by Thomas Cleary. It was from a thrift shop next to an arcade I was patronizing, and they were selling this absolutely adorable version of _The Art of War_. Truly pocket-sized. [I think they still sell them.](https://www.shambhala.com/the-art-of-war-158.html) It was only a dollar (as were all other paperbacks[^thrift]), I wouldn't have bought it otherwise. Pretty boring. I didn't get much out of it.

[^thrift]: **I LOVE THRIFTSTORES!!!!!!!!**

Just before that was Damion Searls's _The Philosophy of Translation_, a marvelous, marvelous book on what translation really is and what it's like to do it. I cannot recommend it enough. It provides some nice perspective amidst all the stupid debates about _The Odyssey_ and anime subtitles by people on Twitter who only speak one language.

I started the year by finishing _Medium Raw_ by Anthony Bourdain. That was an audiobook, narrated by the big man himself. Entertaining as all hell. Listening to his own words in his own voice, it was clear just how far he was from the man who wrote _Kitchen Confidential_ (which I had read the year prior).

At some point in March or February I read _Time War_ (which I loved), _Project Hail Mary_ (which I ***HATED!!!***), and _The Left Hand of Darkness_ (which I absolutely adored). There was that trilogy of books, _Steal Like an Artist_ and the rest, which are very cute reminders of creative advice. To me, they're compasses: nothing that will break new ground, but will assuredly tell you if you're going on the right path or not.

## Remembrance of Things Past

As you can see, not all is lost. I forgot the exact quote, but it's something like "I don't remember every book I've ever read just as I don't remember every meal I've ever had; nevertheless, they are a part of me." Unlike my current predicament, I could just look the exact words up. But to be on theme, I haven't.

That quote's right. The books I've read have already given me something. And if I don't remember a book, it probably wasn't worth remembering. And writing out that list above made me realize just how much I _do_ remember. 

I wish to point out the goal of reading isn't perfect recall or meeting a quota. The goal of reading is to experience, to feel, to read. I went into my NYR knowing that there was a decent chance I wouldn't hit 50 books. But even if I didn't, I still would have read a lot of books. I still would have experienced a lot of things.

No matter what I could have or should have done, the fact remains that I didn't. And now I have a fresh start. Maybe I can look back every now and then but I have to look ahead.  You can only move forward by looking forward. That's what _Danganronpa 2_ taught me.

And anyway, NYRs are kinda silly. A wise, curmudgeonly history teacher of mine once pointed out there's a new year every single day. The 365 days that will pass between today and September 29, 2027 form a year just the same. I could always retry this NYR come 2027, just as so many people do with their own resolutions. But it's a long, long way to Nochevieja, and I have a long, long list of books I want to read.

So I'm making a resolution today: I'm going to read 50 books in a year.

And this time, I'll use _real_ Post-its.
